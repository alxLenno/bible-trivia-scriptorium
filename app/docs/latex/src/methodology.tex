\section{Methodology: The Scriptorium Cycle}
The Scriptorium Framework operates through a recursive Thinking Cycle, visualized in Fig. \ref{fig:cycle}. This cycle ensures that the study remains ``wholesome'' by providing layers of analysis that the user would not typically discover on their own. Following Vanhoozer's \cite{vanhoozer2005} model of ``Theodramatic'' hermeneutics, this methodology views interpretation not as a static data-point, but as a performance of relational truth. Osborne's \cite{osborne2006} hermeneutical spiral provides the structural logic: each pass through the cycle deepens understanding rather than merely repeating it.

\subsection{Layer I: The Linguistic Scalpel (Observation)}
The first layer corresponds to the \textit{Observation} stage of the Inductive Bible Study method \cite{traina1952,bauer2011}. The AI performs a deep semantic domain analysis on key terms, utilizing the taxonomic structures established by Louw and Nida \cite{louwnida1988}. Unlike a simple dictionary lookup, semantic domain analysis groups words by \textit{meaning fields} rather than alphabetical order, revealing relationships between terms that would otherwise remain hidden.

For example, in the study of Philemon, the AI distinguishes between \textit{doulos} (Semantic Domain 87.76: ``slave---one who is in a permanent relation of servitude'') and \textit{diakonos} (Semantic Domain 35.20: ``servant/minister---one who renders service''). This distinction highlights how Paul's deliberate choice of language radically shifts the relational power dynamic from ownership to partnership. The ``Linguistic Scalpel'' cuts through the flattened meaning of modern translations to reveal the emotional and theological ``root intensity'' of the original text.

\subsection{Layer II: The Historical-Critical Shock (Interpretation)}
This layer corresponds to the \textit{Interpretation} stage and provides ``External Shock Data'' to disrupt modern projections \cite{malina2001}. Malina's social-scientific criticism is particularly valuable here, as it forces the reader to abandon anachronistic assumptions about ancient Mediterranean culture. In the Philemon case, the AI provides specific details about Roman law regarding fugitive slaves (\textit{fugitivi}), noting that under the \textit{Lex Fabia} and later \textit{senatus consulta}, harboring a runaway slave was punishable by severe penalties. This ensures the user understands that Paul was not merely asking for a ``favor,'' but was subverting a legal system that carried extreme consequences. This contextual anchoring prevents ``shallow application'' by raising the historical stakes to their proper intensity.

Ricoeur's \cite{ricoeur1976} concept of ``distanciation'' is operative here: the text must first be understood as \textit{other}---as a product of a radically different world---before it can be appropriated into the reader's own horizon. The AI enforces this distanciation by presenting historical data that resists easy domestication.

\subsection{Layer III: The Pauline Bridge (Application)}
Finally, the AI maps these historical and linguistic insights to the user's personal context through the Inductive method \cite{hendricks1991}. By identifying relational archetypes (The Advocate, The Reconciler, The Offender), the AI helps the user ``bridge'' the gap between the 1st and 21st centuries. This is the ``fusion of horizons'' \cite{gadamer1960} in practice: the ancient text's horizon (Roman slavery, patronage, honor-shame culture) is fused with the user's modern horizon (workplace dynamics, family conflict, social justice) to produce a new, actionable understanding.

Fee and Stuart \cite{fee2003} emphasize that application must always flow from sound exegesis, never from isolated proof-texting. The Scriptorium Framework enforces this principle by requiring that every application reference at least one linguistic or historical insight from the preceding layers.
