\section{The Relational Priority: The Father-Child Paradigm}
The Scriptorium Framework is founded on the ``Core Truth'' that scripture reading and understanding should primarily build the relationship between the user and God. As the user moves through the text, the interaction should mirror the relationship between a father and a child \cite{nouwen1992}. This shift from ``I-It'' (mechanical) to ``I-Thou'' (relational) interactions is the framework's defining characteristic \cite{buber1923}.

\subsection{Theological Foundations}
The Father-Child paradigm is not a novel invention but a recovery of a central biblical motif. Paul's use of the Aramaic term \textit{Abba} (Romans 8:15, Galatians 4:6) signifies an intimacy that transcends formal address---it is the language of a child in the presence of a trusted parent. Packer \cite{packer1973} argues that Adoption is the ``highest privilege'' of the Christian life, surpassing even justification, because it establishes a permanent relational status rather than a legal transaction. The Scriptorium Framework operationalizes this priority: every AI interaction must serve the user's sense of being \textit{known by} God, not merely \textit{knowing about} God.

\subsection{Natural Interaction vs. Formal Methodology}
A child does not require a formal methodology to approach their father. The interaction happens naturally, driven by love and a desire to know the father's heart. Most contemporary Bible study methods impose a ``barrier of technique,'' where the user feels they must master a system before they can hear from God. Fee and Stuart \cite{fee2003} acknowledge this tension in their influential guide, noting that while exegetical tools are necessary, they must never become ``ends in themselves.'' The Scriptorium Framework addresses this by embedding the tools \textit{within} the conversation, so that the user encounters a Greek word root or a historical fact as a natural part of dialogue rather than as a prerequisite assignment.

\subsection{The AI as a Relational Facilitator}
In this framework, the AI's role is not to enforce a method, but to facilitate a conversation. The ``Stretching Rule'' and ``Socratic Rule'' (detailed in Section 5) are designed to act like a mentor who gently guides a child toward deeper questions, not to grade their performance. The success of a study session is measured not by the accuracy of the summary, but by the user's articulated sense of relational growth.

Vygotsky's \cite{vygotsky1978} concept of the ``Zone of Proximal Development'' (ZPD) is directly applicable here. The AI operates as the ``More Knowledgeable Other'' (MKO), providing scaffolding that enables the user to reach interpretive insights they could not achieve alone. Critically, the scaffolding is removed once the user demonstrates independent understanding, preventing the AI from becoming a permanent crutch. Sch\"{o}n's \cite{schon1983} model of reflective practice further supports this: the AI prompts ``reflection-in-action'' by surfacing contradictions and asking the user to reconcile them in real time.
